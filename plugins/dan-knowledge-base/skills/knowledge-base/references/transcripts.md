# Reading a meeting transcript

A meeting file holds the recap the meeting tool wrote and, below it, the
verbatim transcript. Whoever reads it — a covered gate writing a digest, or the
session where there is no gate — is at the step where a meeting's facts are most
easily bent, and the bends read as plausibly as the facts, so this is how to
read one.

## Why the transcript is verbatim

Measured on 15 meetings in November 2026: Fathom's recap carried about 56% of a
transcript's substantive facts. A model-written compaction of the transcript
kept more, but misattributed speakers, reversed who asked whom, and firmed up
hedges, at roughly three statements a meeting on Sonnet and six on Haiku. The
verbatim transcript kept every fact. So nothing upstream of this reading
condenses it, and the reading is where the care goes.

## The recap is a map and a second opinion

Use the recap to find the subjects and as a check on your own reading. It is
also what the attendees were sent, so it is what they believe was said. Where it
and the transcript disagree, the transcript wins, and the disagreement often
marks a garbled passage worth reading twice. The recap is never the source for
who said or decided something.

## Speaker labels are unreliable

A label is often the device or account that joined rather than the person
speaking, and turns from several people get merged under one label. Attribute a
statement or a commitment to a person only when the content confirms it: they
are addressed by name, or they refer to themselves. Otherwise attribute it to
the meeting ("the meeting agreed", "it was raised that").

## Keep the hedge

"Maybe", "I think", "we could" and questions are not decisions or commitments.
Record them as tentative or open, in those words, rather than as what was
decided.

## Do not resolve garble

A garbled passage is left out or marked unclear, never turned into a confident
fact by guessing what it must have meant. Proper nouns are the usual casualty:
check a name against the source's list of known misspellings before recording
it, and before concluding a company is new.

## Check direction

Who asks whom, who sends what to whom, and which side "we" and "they" mean are
the facts a reader inverts most easily, and an inverted one is worse than a
missing one. Confirm each from the surrounding turns before writing it down.

## Hypotheticals are not events

An example name, client or number used to illustrate a point did not happen.
"Say a member at a client like X calls in" records nothing about X.

## Similar names can be different people

Two colleagues whose names differ by a letter, or a first name shared by a
colleague and a contact, are separate people until the content says otherwise.
Do not merge them.

## Numbers and dates

Copy a number or a date only when it is unambiguous in the transcript. Speech
recognition renders "fifteen" and "fifty" alike and turns spoken dates into
odd forms; where the transcript leaves room for doubt, leave the number out or
say it was unclear.
